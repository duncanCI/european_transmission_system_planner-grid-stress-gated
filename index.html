Method & limits (read before quoting)

Not NTC. Headroom is the GW of ONE shaped transfer an otherwise unloaded network absorbs before the first post-fault thermal limit. There is no dispatch base case — a corridor loaded in every credible market hour shows the same headroom as an idle one. Not NTC, TTC or boundary capability.

N-1 of what. Single outage of each modelled AC branch (line record or ≥220 kV transformer) within its synchronous area. No generator or busbar contingencies. Multi-circuit corridors are collapsed into one record carrying the SUM of the circuits’ ratings and their parallel reactance — 27.8% of in-scope spans (20,360 of 73,234) have two or more circuits. Outaging one such record therefore removes every circuit on the corridor at once: that is a common-tower double-circuit contingency, and it is more severe than a single-circuit N-1, not less. An earlier version of this note claimed the opposite; treat multi-circuit corridors as pessimistic on the outage and optimistic on the intact rating, and check n_circuits before quoting either.

Two stress mechanisms, reported separately. Bulk transfer stress (cross-border, internal and offshore-landing shapes) is what boundary reinforcement addresses. DC converter swing is a local phenomenon, and per GW an injection at one converter bus in a weak network always out-stresses a spatially distributed 1 GW transfer — so a single combined ranking is dominated by DC (in GB, 49 of the top 50 branches, 27 of them from one link). The selector above switches between them; "worst of both" is the conservative envelope. The GB validation gate is run against the bulk transfer figures, because that is the like-for-like comparison with a published boundary map — the DC mechanism is screened and reported, not ranked against something it does not describe. For a DC-swing figure, compare it against the link's own rating: a branch that binds at 0.55 GW of swing on a 1.2 GW link is exposed to a credible single event.

DC links & interconnectors. Each v23 DC link ≥500 MW enters as a 1 GW converter-injection transfer pattern: embedded links as end-to-end swings, interconnector and offshore landings as infeed against the national demand shape (collinear links landing at the same converter bus — bipole halves, converter clusters — share one pattern). Per GW, the AC-flow consequence of a schedule swing and of a full-import outage is the same linear pattern, so one pattern measures both — with the counterbalance proxied by the same national shape (real post-fault balancing is reserve-distributed across the synchronous area; screening proxy). Combined cases are screened: every AC branch outage is evaluated under every DC pattern. DC links carry no scheduled flow and provide no relief — corridors paralleled by embedded HVDC are shown conservatively (worse than operational reality).

Envelope colors. Each branch shows its own worst case over all shaped transfer directions and all outages — neighbouring reds need not bind together. Click for the binding pair.

Scope. ≥220 kV, 50 Hz AC per synchronous area. The grey linework beneath is the full 50–750 kV dataset, context only — unscreened. The "with plans" network contains national and TSO development-plan circuits and ENTSO-E TYNDP 2026 AC transmission projects at their published commissioning years, so the plan case reflects both national and pan-European build. TYNDP DC projects are not branches — DC enters as converter-swing patterns.

Where planned lines land. A development plan names a place, not a busbar. Where the endpoint is certain (the plan names a station we hold) the circuit connects there. Where it is not, the endpoint is treated as probabilistic: the circuit is split across the two or three major terminals that plausibly host it — scored by distance, whether the node is a real substation rather than a tee-point, voltage match and connectivity — with admittance and rating divided by weight, so each plausible terminal gets partial relief instead of one arbitrary terminal getting all of it. This is an expected network, not a scenario ensemble: a real build lands the whole circuit on one terminal, and a fan creates a path between its own candidate terminals that no single realisation has (candidates are held within 30 km of each other to bound it). Every branch popup carries the resolution it used. The previous release required an exact station match at both ends and silently dropped 29% of line-like schemes — which understated the relief the plans bring.

Shaping. Transfers are shaped by TYNDP national totals mapped to today's OSM plant sites and population-weighted demand, plus per-country offshore-wind landing patterns and per-converter DC swing patterns; future new-build sites and large point loads are not represented.

Ratings. DC MW flow vs static typed continuous MVA rating (pf ≈ 1); no seasonal or short-term post-fault emergency ratings — conservative vs operational practice.

What has been validated, and what has not. Great Britain only, against NESO's published material. Direction: of our 50 most transfer-stressed meshed corridors, 92% fall within 10 km of a NESO "Beyond 2030" reinforcement corridor, against a 72.7% random baseline (permutation p = 0.0005). Magnitude: across 138 routes matched to ETYS 2025 Appendix B, per-circuit reactance tracks the published values (Spearman 0.754, median ratio 0.895), and our three most-stressed boundaries — B6, B7a, B8 — are exactly NESO's three largest published 2030 capability shortfalls. Boundary ranking correlation is weaker (Spearman 0.419 over 17 boundaries, p = 0.09) — treat the boundary order as indicative, not as a ranking to quote. No continental cross-check has been done: outside GB, direction and magnitude are unvalidated, and the further from a meshed 400 kV core you go the less the screening assumptions hold.
